## Introduction

Cells are characterized by rich, high‑dimensional transcriptional programs. Deep neural networks can learn predictive structure in these data, but conventional multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) sacrifice precise mechanistic interpretability for performance: nonlinear interactions are distributed across layers, obscuring how input genes combine to drive outputs. Bilinear MLPs seek a middle ground. They preserve nonlinear representational capacity while yielding an explicit, algebraically tractable description of how *pairs* of input genes interact for each output target. This post summarizes exploratory work applying bilinear MLPs to three single‑cell tasks: (1) cell type classification, (2) transcriptional scale (graph frequency) regression, and (3) RNA velocity regression. We emphasize interpretability via learned gene–gene interaction matrices and their eigendecompositions, presented as hypothesis‑generating tools rather than definitive biological claims.


---

## Data & Preprocessing

All demonstrations use the developing mouse pancreas endocrinogenesis dataset (`scvelo.datasets.pancreas`). Standard preprocessing: total count normalization, log1p transform, selection of the top 10,000 highly variable genes (ribosomal genes with prefixes Rpl/Rps removed). For classification we used 3,696 cells spanning eight annotated endocrine and progenitor types. Train/validation splits were random (70/30), non‑stratified (important caveat for rare classes). Frequency regression targets were derived from Laplacian eigenvectors of a kNN graph (n_neighbors=15) built in PCA space. Velocity regression targets used RNA velocity estimates (scVelo) on spliced/unspliced layers.

## Methods

### Architecture

Let $$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^g$$ be a gene expression vector (g = number of selected genes). A single bilinear layer applies two linear maps
$$
L(\mathbf{x}) = W_l \mathbf{x}
$$
and
$$
R(\mathbf{x}) = W_r \mathbf{x}
$$
producing hidden representations whose element‑wise (Hadamard) product $$\mathbf{h} = L(\mathbf{x}) \odot R(\mathbf{x})$$ captures multiplicative (i.e. pairwise) gating. An output head for target dimension $$i$$ applies a weight vector $$\mathbf{w}_{p,i}$$, yielding prediction
$$
\hat{y}_i = \mathbf{w}_{p,i}^{\top} (W_l \mathbf{x} \odot W_r \mathbf{x}).
$$
Expanding gives a bilinear form
$$
\hat{y}_i = \mathbf{x}^{\top} \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{x},
$$
where interaction matrix $$\mathbf{Q}_i$$ is assembled from learned weights (symmetrized for analysis). Thus each output has an explicit gene–gene interaction matrix.

### Interpretability

For each target $i$, eigendecomposition $\mathbf{Q}_i = V \Lambda V^{\top}$ provides orthogonal gene modules (columns of $V$) ranked by associated eigenvalues (magnitude indicates contribution to the quadratic form). Signs of eigenvectors are arbitrary; “positive” / “negative” gene sets are descriptive conveniences. High‑loading genes in leading eigenvectors are candidate coordinated interaction modules informative for the target. We report top modules and enrichment patterns (exploratory only).

### Tasks

1. Cell Type Classification: Softmax over eight pancreatic cell identities; cross‑entropy loss.
2. Frequency Regression: Predict coefficients of selected graph Laplacian eigenvectors ("frequencies") describing transcriptional length scales over the cell–cell graph.
3. Velocity Regression: Predict per‑gene RNA velocity estimates; mean squared error loss.

## Results

### Cell type classification

Single bilinear model (hidden size 128, 100 epochs, Adam, cosine annealing, strong weight decay 0.35). Validation accuracy: 89.0% (training 98.8%; generalization gap 9.8%). Random baseline ≈12.5%. Performance varied by class (non‑stratified split): Ngn3 low EP (96.2%), Pre‑endocrine (95.6%), Alpha (92.7%), Ductal (91.8%), Epsilon (90.3%), Ngn3 high EP (84.2%), Beta (72.3%), Delta (38.9%). Lower accuracy for rare / transcriptionally overlapping types (Delta, Beta).

### Frequency regression

Model predicts coefficients for selected graph Laplacian eigenvectors ("spatial frequencies") of the cell–cell similarity graph. Lower frequencies emphasize broad developmental gradients; higher frequencies capture localized transcriptional contrasts (e.g. emerging hormone identities or cell cycle exit). Mean absolute error (MAE) example run: 0.0025 (reported). Gene interaction matrices for low‑frequency targets showed modules enriched in metabolic / differentiation programs; higher frequency targets highlighted hormone maturation and cell cycle control. Interpretation remains speculative pending rigorous benchmarking.

## Limitations

- Single random, non‑stratified split; no independent test set.
- No baseline comparisons (e.g. logistic regression, linear models, shallow MLP) yet.
- Strong weight decay (0.35) chosen heuristically; may bias interaction structure.
- Eigenvector signs arbitrary; module polarity interchangeable.
- Enrichment background not restricted to expressed / variable genes (potential bias).
- Stability, permutation, and reproducibility analyses not performed.
- Frequency and velocity targets depend on upstream graph / velocity pipeline choices.
- Correlational: interaction matrices do not imply causal gene regulation.

---

## Derivation

Relation to gated linear units (GLUs): a bilinear layer can be viewed as a GLU without a sigmoid nonlinearity, where both projected gene features $(W_l \mathbf{x})_k$ and $(W_r \mathbf{x})_k$ must be simultaneously high to contribute strongly through their product. Expanding the output head over element‑wise products yields the bilinear form $\mathbf{x}^{\top} \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{x}$, linking predictions directly to pairwise gene interactions. Symmetrization $\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{Q}_i + \mathbf{Q}_i^{\top})$ ensures real eigen decomposition for module extraction.

---

## Conclusion

Bilinear MLPs provide an explicit mapping from outputs to quadratic gene–gene interaction forms, enabling post‑hoc eigendecomposition into gene modules for multiple single‑cell tasks. Early exploratory results suggest: (1) classification performance competitive for most pancreatic cell types while revealing interaction modules aligned with developmental and secretory programs, (2) frequency regression may disentangle transcriptional scales, and (3) velocity regression highlights opposing transcriptional forces. However, methodological and statistical limitations currently preclude strong biological claims. Next steps include baseline benchmarking, stability analyses (bootstrapping / weight resampling), permutation tests for interaction significance, stratified data splits, and integration with pathway‑constrained priors. These refinements will clarify whether bilinear interpretability adds durable value beyond conventional models.
